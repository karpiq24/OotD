Along the road stand five small, freshly built stone shrines in a row, each dedicated to a different hero: one crowned with a pair of great curved bull horns and stacked with offerings of beer cups (by far the most decorated and most visited), one hung with a wizard's star emblems, one with a golden spear, one with an ornate gauntlet, one with a bow and a wooden star-chart. Villagers kneel and leave bread, coins and flowers.

At the largest shrine, a young robed teacher demonstrates a tiny harmless cantrip — a floating mote of light — to a ring of fascinated peasant children, while an older figure in academy robes watches from the roadside and quietly notes down the names of the children who show a spark.

In the middle distance, a grand classical temple that once held five divine statues: four of the niches now stand empty with only faded outlines and memorial plaques where the statues were, while the fifth — a serene dawn-goddess in white and rose-gold — has been moved to the center and is freshly garlanded. Priests carry the other four statues out in a slow, respectful procession.
